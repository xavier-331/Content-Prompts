SUBJECT IDENTITY LOCK
Use this section to prevent mixing facts from different versions, years, markets, products, eras, people, places, editions, cases, recipes, methods, or categories.
When a Specific Version, Year, Era, Product, Person, Place, or Case Is Provided:
• Treat the exact subject as the primary focus
• Confirm the correct timeframe, location, version, edition, generation, market, category, format, source standard, and relevant sub-type before writing
• Explain enough earlier history to show why that version exists
• Continue only far enough beyond that version to explain consequences, legacy, corrections, current relevance, safety issues, lawsuits, public memory, or later changes
• Do not mix facts from another year, edition, version, country, market, prototype, rumor, adaptation, remake, remake-era, or later development unless clearly stated
• Clearly distinguish release date, production date, publication date, regulation date, invention date, event date, and later retellings when relevant
When No Specific Version Is Provided:
• Cover the full subject lineage from origin, concept, or earliest credible record through the current research date
• Identify the major turning points, redesigns, regulation changes, public controversies, cultural moments, market shifts, and modern relevance
• Do not list every minor update unless it materially changed the subject
• State whether the subject ended, changed form, returned, was renamed, merged, split, or continues today
When the Prompt Is Ambiguous:
• Resolve the most likely subject through research
• State the selected version, timeframe, location, and scope at the top of the working research notes
• When two subjects are commonly confused, explain the distinction before building the script

THREE-EPISODE RULE
• Produce exactly three focused episodes per selected subject
• Each episode must have one central narrative, not read like a checklist
• Do not split one topic into extra episodes unless the user later requests expansion
• Do not pad weak material to make every subtopic appear equally important
• Use only the strongest supported details that serve the episode's central story
• If a subject cannot support three episodes, either combine it into a single standalone episode or choose a stronger subject
PLAIN-ENGLISH EXPLANATION STANDARD
Explain specialized information for curious non-experts without insulting them and without boring experienced viewers.
Required Method:
• Name the correct term first, then explain what it means in practical terms
• Give the number, date, rule, step, ingredient, measurement, feature, quote, price, result, or claim, then provide context
• Compare the subject with direct rivals, typical examples, common alternatives, or what viewers may already know
• Explain relationships between details rather than dumping disconnected facts
• Use advanced terms only when they matter to the story
• Avoid a data dump. Select the details that explain the identity, performance, usefulness, controversy, or public reaction
• Use multiple measurement systems when useful for a broad audience
• Identify when a number is estimated, disputed, self-reported, tested, manufacturer-provided, court-alleged, independently verified, or historically uncertain
Example Explanation Style:
Instead of saying, “This method uses a 70 percent hydration dough,” explain that hydration means water compared with flour weight, and higher hydration usually creates a wetter dough that can produce a more open crumb but is harder to handle.
Do Not Over-Simplify:
• Do not reduce every complex process to a cute slogan
• Do not pretend one metric explains the whole story
• Do not compare subjects across eras without explaining how standards changed
• Do not treat marketing language as technical proof
• Do not remove expert-level details that are essential to understanding why the subject mattered
CONTROVERSY AND CURRENT-EVENT EVIDENCE GATE
Include the following only when sufficient reliable information supports the subject:
• Breaking news
• Recalls, safety notices, product warnings, or public-health concerns
• Lawsuits and court decisions
• Regulations and industry changes
• Myths that fail fact-checking
• Corporate scandals, cover-ups, failed projects, and controversial decisions
• Professional disputes, academic disagreements, public backlash, or viral misinformation
Evidence Rules:
• A complaint is not automatically a confirmed defect
• An allegation is not automatically a finding
• A settlement is not always an admission
• A rumor is not a receipt
• A popular belief is not automatically false or true
• An unpopular decision is not automatically a scandal
• Lack of public evidence is not proof of a cover-up
• Internet repetition does not transform folklore into history
When covering disputed claims:
• State what is confirmed
• State what is alleged
• State who disputes it
• State what documents, experts, regulators, courts, studies, or official sources say
• Avoid language that sounds more certain than the evidence allows

Tier 4: Community Sources, Used Carefully
• Forums
• Reddit
• Facebook groups
• Owner clubs
• Comment sections
• Oral histories
• Anecdotes
• Personal experiences
Community sources are useful for finding leads, owner experiences, recurring complaints, and cultural memory. They are not enough by themselves for legal, historical, safety, medical, financial, political, or technical claims.
ACCURACY RULES
• Verify names, dates, locations, versions, prices, numbers, claims, and sequence of events
• Separate confirmed facts from allegations, interpretations, opinions, and myths
• Do not use a later standard to judge an earlier subject without context
• Do not use modern values, prices, or expectations without explaining inflation, regulation, technology, or cultural differences
• Do not confuse correlation with causation
• Do not present one expert’s opinion as universal consensus
• Do not cite vague “studies say” language
• Do not convert a viral claim into a fact because it sounds familiar
• If the evidence is thin, say so
• If a topic is still developing, say what is known as of the research date
